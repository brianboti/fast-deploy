# After the release: monitoring, decisions and reports. Fast Deploy 1.25.2 against research and practice

Written 2026-10-21 UTC (the evening of 2026-10-20, Central time) as a read-only review. Nothing in the code was changed.
The question asked was the operator's: once an application is deployed, the platform must be automated and trustworthy
about what happens next. It must gather numbers, decide on them where it safely can, and otherwise report back in simple
words and gently. Where does it stand today, and what do the best current practices say it should do?

Method: the code on `main` (commit `9289b09`, version 1.25.2; the one commit after 1.25.1 changes only the settings page) was read in full where it gathers, judges or reports:
the bake (`labdeploy/bake.py`), the release runner (`labdeploy/release.py`), the live views (`labdeploy/cloud.py`,
`labdeploy/telemetry.py`), the status and its diagnosis (`labdeploy/status.py`), the chat's code-written answers
(`labdeploy/chat.py`), the cost report (`labdeploy/cost.py`), the controller's background jobs (`labdeploy/scheduler.py`,
the watchdog in `labdeploy/controller.py`), the Terraform that creates the uptime checks, alerts, objective and dashboard
(`infra/gcp/modules/service/main.tf`, `slo.tf`, `infra/gcp/modules/project/`), the five workflows that run on a schedule,
and the runbook, chat guide and validation record. Then the live state was read, never changed: the alert policies,
channels, uptime checks and objectives in both projects through the Monitoring API, the budgets, the native spend caps,
the scheduled runs and the living issues on GitHub, and which secrets and variables exist. Six themed searches then ran in
parallel (release verification and automatic rollback; objectives, error budgets and alerting for small services; the
observability stack and AI-assisted operations; what a trustworthy report contains; cost monitoring and anomalies; safe
deployment practices, health gates, drills and watching the watchers), each returning only what it had fetched. Section 8
marks each source as read on the official page or in the source file (F), seen in a secondary source (S) or unverified
(U).

Acronyms: SLO (service level objective: a target such as "99.5 percent of requests answered without a server error over
30 days"), SLI (the measured indicator behind it), error budget (the share the objective lets you miss), burn rate (how
fast that budget is being used), p95 (the latency that 95 percent of requests beat), DORA (DevOps Research and
Assessment, Google's yearly study of delivery performance), IAP (Identity-Aware Proxy, Google's sign-in front door),
OTel (OpenTelemetry, the open standard for traces, metrics and logs), SDP (Microsoft's safe deployment practices).

## 1. The short answer

**The platform is strong in the ten minutes around a release and thin in the days after it.** Measured against the
operator's three verbs, gather, decide, report:

- **Gather: good at the release, partial afterwards.** The bake reads the new revision's real requests and system log
  once a minute; the health view reads Cloud Run's own metrics, the uptime check and the objective; the weekly report
  reads 14 days of usage and the bill. All of it comes from Google's free, built-in telemetry, which the research
  confirms is the right source for single-container services (section 5.3). What is not gathered: anything about a
  release after its five-minute bake, a before-and-after per release, the spend between one Monday and the next, and
  the platform's own track record (how many releases fail, how fast they recover, how often the bake was wrong).
- **Decide: right where it decides, and the fence is in the right place.** The platform acts on its own in exactly the
  three places the industry says a machine should: restoring the previous traffic when a release fails its rules,
  closing the front door and stopping databases at the budget line, and refusing a plan that breaks a policy. It never
  deletes, never repairs drift, never applies a right-sizing proposal, never merges a contract by itself without a
  person's standing permission. That is the shape Google, Amazon, Microsoft and the recent writing on AI operations all
  describe (sections 5.1 and 5.6): automatic for the reversible and bounded, a person for the rest, with every action
  recorded. The two decision rules that need work are the bake's verdict on a quiet service, which passes on no evidence
  and is then never revisited, and the production objective, which a handful of errors on a quiet application spends
  for a month.
- **Report: the words are right, the delivery is not.** Every chat answer is written by code from the numbers, in one
  shape, in plain words, with a **Next** line; that is the consensus design for a model in the loop (section 5.3), and
  the tone already matches what the style guides ask for (section 5.4). But the platform never speaks first. Every
  automatic signal, 89 alert policies and the budget's thresholds, ends in one mailbox; no webhook or chat channel is
  configured anywhere; the watchdog's daily findings, the follow job's results, the weekly reports and the drift check
  wait in a status tool or a GitHub issue until someone asks; a production promotion sends nothing at all; and there is
  no "all quiet" message, so silence cannot be told from a stopped watcher. The evidence on alert fatigue says a mailbox
  with 80 policies for 13 services, eight of them test services, and an alert on any ERROR log line, will be ignored.

So the honest position: the platform already gathers the right numbers and decides the right things at release time;
what the next phase should build is the quiet, regular voice after it, a before-and-after for each release, an
objective that survives a quiet month, the cost view between Mondays, and the few numbers that let the operator see
whether the automation deserves the trust it is given. None of it needs a new product, a sidecar, a tracing stack or a
paid plan; all of it fits inside Google's free allotments, with one date to plan for (section 5.5). Section 6 ranks it.

Three things the operator can do today, at no cost, before any code changes: create production's native spend cap
(`scripts/spend-cap.sh create fast-deploy-prod-c602 20`; staging has one, production does not), turn on Google's free
Cost Anomaly Detection for the billing account with the daily-summary email, and give the alerts a second channel (a
Slack or Discord webhook in the foundation's `alert_webhook_url` and the controller's `notify_webhook_url`), so that
something other than the mailbox hears them.

## 2. Where the platform stands

The platform already gathers numbers at four moments and reports through five channels. Read as a whole, it is a
release-verification system with alerts bolted on: strong for the ten minutes around a release, thin for the days after.

### 2.1 During a release: gather, judge, act

| Moment | What is measured | What decides | What a person sees |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before traffic moves | The candidate revision at its no-traffic tag: Cloud Run's startup probe, then the platform's own HTTP calls to the health path with a signed token | A failed probe stops the release; nothing new ever serves; the candidate's last 50 log lines go into the receipt | "The new version failed; visitors stayed on the old one", with the log lines and, since 1.24.0, a diagnosis: the cause in one line, the line the developer changes, the contract key the operator changes (seven documented failure messages: wrong port, loopback bind, out of memory, probe failure, gunicorn worker timeout, missing module, `EACCES`) |
| After traffic moves (staging: all at once; production: 10, 50, then 100 percent) | The bake, five minutes by default (up to 15): the new revision's own request logs and system log, read once a minute. Three rules (decision D22): a crash or restart; more than 5 percent 5xx among at least 20 requests; p95 latency above 3 times the previous revision's over the hour before, when both saw 20 requests. The health path is left out of both rates | Errors and crashes stop the bake at once; the previous traffic split comes back automatically; a production step that fails names the share ("with 10 percent of the traffic"). Too little traffic is `inconclusive` and passes; unreadable logs are `unobserved` and pass loudly | "Watched 5 min with real traffic: 85 requests, 0 server errors" or "too little traffic to judge, so only the health checks vouch for it"; the receipt holds the window's numbers, the rules and up to five crash lines |
| The run itself | Every GitHub job and step with its outcome and duration; the receipt's phases with timestamps | The controller records the final state once, re-reads a run GitHub marked complete too early for six hours, and a background job follows every release nobody is watching (`follow_tick`) | One bold line, a few facts, one **Next** line, written by code from the result, never by the model |

Proven live: a bake that passed on 85 requests, one that restored traffic 72 seconds after promotion (4 of 20 answers
5xx), one inconclusive on zero traffic (1.9.0, 2026-10-14); a production fault caught at 10 percent after 69 seconds
(16 of 31 answers 5xx) and the fix passing 10, 50 and 100 on real traffic; a rollback by tool in 16 seconds (1.18.3,
2026-10-18). The diagnosis of 1.24.0 is tested but has not been exercised live yet.

### 2.2 After a release, continuously: the cloud watches

Every application root creates, in Terraform and inside Google's free allotment:

| Watch | Rule | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| Uptime check on the health path | Staging every 5 minutes from every region; production every minute from Oregon, Iowa and Virginia; signed as the Monitoring service agent (pages for people accept the sign-in redirect) | "health check failing": more than one region failing for 5 minutes |
| Server errors | More than 5 answers 5xx in five minutes (`error_alert_count`, 1 to 10,000) | alert |
| Slow responses | p95 above 2,000 ms for five minutes (`latency_alert_ms`) | alert |
| At maximum instances | 3 or more active instances for five minutes (`instance_alert_count`) | alert |
| Memory | p95 utilization above 80 percent for five minutes | alert |
| Errors in the application log | any log line at ERROR or worse; at most one notification an hour | alert |
| Production only: the objective | 99.5 percent of requests without a server error over a rolling 30 days, counted from Cloud Run's request metric with the uptime check's calls included; burn-rate alerts at 10 times the sustainable rate over 60 minutes ("fast", act now) and 2 times over 24 hours ("slow", today), with Google's starting points | alert, with a documentation block that names the tools to use and the runbook section |
| A dashboard per service | Requests by response class, p95 latency, active instances, memory, CPU, billable time | the Cloud console |
| The spending cap | Budget notifications at 50, 90, 100 percent and 100 percent forecast go to a topic; a Cloud Workflow closes ingress on every managed service and stops every managed database at the threshold (1.0 of the budget in both projects), optionally detaches billing above it; a cap execution that fails raises its own alert. Google's native Cloud Run spend cap is a second, independent layer | the budget email, the failure alert, `get_controller_status` |
| The controller itself | `/healthz` every minute from three regions with a content match and certificate check; a GitHub workflow pings it every 30 minutes and fails loudly | "Fast Deploy controller: not answering"; GitHub's failed-workflow email |

Every alert closes itself after 30 minutes without the condition.

### 2.3 On demand: what Claude can read

The controller holds a read-only identity for the cloud (`Observer`). `get_service_health` returns, for the last 60
minutes by default: whether Cloud Run reports the service ready, which revision serves and whether it is the release the
controller recorded, requests, 5xx answers, p95 latency, the busiest instance count, p95 memory, the uptime check's pass
rate (which sees an outage the service's own metrics cannot, such as a cap that closed the front door), the declared
needs and whether each exists, who may open the pages, and in production the objective with its remaining error budget.
`get_recent_logs` returns the newest lines at a severity. `get_cost_report` returns the latest weekly report.
`get_controller_status` returns the background jobs' last results, the watchdog's findings, the drift check's newest
result, the spend caps recorded, the GitHub credential's expiry, and what follows a platform release. `list_events` is
the audit trail.

Every one of these comes back with a `summary` that code writes from the result: one bold line, two to five facts, one
**Next** line, so the words and the numbers are the same whoever asks and are never the model's invention. The health
summary's logic is fixed: not ready, else errors in the window, else uptime pass rate under 99 percent, else healthy;
trouble ends in "look at the error lines; if the latest release caused it, roll back". Claude's playbook (the skill)
tells it to keep that shape, use plain words, never call a check passed unless the status says so, and quote the tools'
numbers rather than prices from memory.

## 4. The gaps, against "gather, decide, report gently"

Each gap names what the code does today, what the evidence says, and how it was measured.

**G1. The bake has no verdict for most releases, and nobody looks later.** A release with fewer than 20 requests
besides the health path in its five minutes is `inconclusive` and passes; the validation record shows this is the
usual case (the no-traffic release of 1.9.0; every step of the two quiet production promotions of 2026-10-18 and
2026-10-19). The choice to pass is defensible, and it is what Datadog's metric-based objectives do with missing data;
Flagger's choice is the opposite and Argo's is to pause for a person (section 5.1). What no tool does is forget: the
industry's bakes run for hours and days and roll back on an alarm after the switch (Amazon, Microsoft, Meta), and the
monitoring products compute a before-and-after per release once enough time and traffic have passed (New Relic,
Datadog). Here, once the five minutes end, the release is `succeeded` for ever; an error that starts an hour later is
an alert in the mailbox with no link to the release that caused it. Measured: the runtime alerts, the health view and
the status have no notion of "since the last release".

**G2. The production objective cannot survive a quiet month.** 99.5 percent over 30 days on an application with a few
thousand requests a month allows a handful of errors; the rollback drill's 18 spent it and the health view said
"spent" for weeks (section 3). The fast-burn alert has no short window and `duration 0s`, so one bad five-minute
bucket can notify, which Google's own page warns about for low traffic (section 5.2). The practitioners' answers are
known and codable: gate the page on a minimum number of requests, show "no requests" instead of 100 percent, keep the
budget honest but say when it recovers, and give the uptime check its own objective instead of mixing its calls into
the users' one (section 5.2). Measured: the live objective and its two alerts in production, and the health view's
`budget_line`.

**G3. One mailbox, no severity, no routing, and more noise than signal.** 89 policies in two projects go to one
email channel; none carries a severity or a user label; the "errors in the application log" policy fires on any
ERROR line (once an hour at most); 60 of staging's 80 policies and 10 of its 13 uptime checks belong to test or trial
services from earlier phases; the budget emails and Google's own notices land in the same inbox. The evidence says such
a mailbox is ignored, and the records show no alert email was ever confirmed read (section 3). Measured: the Monitoring
API listing, the repositories' secrets, the validation record.

**G4. The platform never speaks first.** The controller's watchdog, follow job, drift view and platform-update view
all write findings that wait to be asked for; the one push path, `notify_webhook_url`, is unset, as is
`NOTIFY_WEBHOOK_URL` in every repository; `promote.yml` has no notification job at all. The chat, where the operator
is, cannot be pushed to, so the outward channel must be something else: email, a webhook, or a GitHub issue that
notifies its watchers. Industry practice is "always send notifications at the end of each deployment", a digest rather
than one message per event, an explicit all-clear so that silence is never mistaken for health, and a heartbeat whose
absence is the alarm (sections 5.4 and 5.6). Measured: the controller's code, the secrets listing, the workflows.

**G5. No before-and-after.** The health view is an absolute 60-minute window ("healthy" means no errors now); the
receipt holds the bake's window only; nothing compares the day after a release with the week before it. AWS calls the
missing piece "operations metrics baselines"; Meta found comparison thresholds catch more than static ones; New Relic
and Datadog compute the change per deployment (sections 5.1 and 5.4). The data are one API call away: Cloud Run's
metrics carry the revision's name. Measured: `labdeploy/cloud.py` and `labdeploy/chat.py`.

**G6. The diagnosis stops at the candidate.** Seven of Google's roughly 39 documented failure messages are mapped,
and only for a failed candidate. A service that passed and then fails hours later (no available instance, a memory
kill, the request timeout, a cap that closed it) produces a raw alert and a health view whose **Next** line says to
look at the error lines and roll back. The same table, applied to the recent log lines and written into each alert's
documentation block, would say the cause in a sentence (sections 5.2 and 5.3). Measured: `labdeploy/status.py`.

**G7. Cost is watched weekly and at the budget line, with nothing between.** The weekly report is thorough; the cap
acts. But a leak that starts on Tuesday is seen the next Monday or at 50 percent of the budget, whichever comes first;
the report compares with nothing (no previous week, no forecast against the budget); Google's free anomaly detector,
generally available since October 2025, is not mentioned anywhere in the platform; production has no native spend
cap; and a "$50 Monthly Budget Alert" of unknown origin sits on the billing account. On the FinOps ladder this is
"crawl" for anomalies and reporting (section 5.5). Measured: the budgets listing, `scripts/spend-cap.sh check`, the
report code.

**G8. Trust is not measured.** Every number the industry uses to decide how much to trust automation exists in the
controller's database and nowhere else: releases and their final states, restores by the bake, rollbacks by a person,
the time from a failure to the next success, the share of inconclusive bakes. Nothing computes a change failure rate,
a recovery time or a bake false-positive rate, so "automated and trustable" cannot be shown, only felt. Elastic's
rule, widen autonomy only after measuring acceptance and rollback rates, and the SRE workbook's precision and recall of
alerts both need these counts (sections 5.4 and 5.6). Measured: `labdeploy/controller.py` and the plans table.

**G9. Drills are scheduled on paper.** The runbook sets the rollback and cap-fire drills twice a year, the restore
drill quarterly and "prove the alerts work" monthly; only the restore drill has a reminder and a record, and no run of
the alert proof is recorded anywhere. Google's teams drill weekly; AWS names "documented but never exercised" as the
anti-pattern (section 5.6). Measured: `restore_drill_reminder` and the events table.

**G10. Some watchers are unwatched.** The drift report's age is checked (stale after eight days); the cost report's is
not; the daily watchdog has no heartbeat of its own; GitHub disables a public repository's schedules after 60 idle days
and emails a scheduled run's failure only to whoever last edited the schedule (section 5.6). The controller itself is
watched well (uptime check with certificate validation, plus the GitHub ping every 30 minutes). Measured: the watchdog
code and GitHub's documentation.

## 5. What the industry does

### 5.1 Verifying a release and deciding to roll back

**The tools agree on the shape the platform already has, and differ on what "no data" means.** Argo Rollouts runs an
analysis at an interval (the documentation's example: every five minutes, pass when the success rate is at least 0.95,
fail after three bad measurements), tolerates no failed measurement by default, and, the point that matters here, treats
a measurement that meets neither condition as inconclusive, which "causes a rollout to become paused at its current
step" until a person resumes or aborts it (F). Flagger checks every 60 seconds with built-in rules of at least 99
percent non-5xx answers and a p99 under 500 ms, rolls back after a configurable number of failed checks, and in its source
counts "no values found ... probably not receiving traffic" as a failed check (F); its documented fix for a quiet service
is a load-tester webhook that sends traffic during the analysis (S). Kayenta, the Netflix and Google judge, compares a
canary with a baseline deployed at the same moment, never with "before", scores 0 to 100, sends a marginal score (75 to
95) "to a human approval path", and wants "at least 50 pieces of time series data per metric", a three-hour lifetime
and hourly intervals; a run with half its metrics lacking data scores zero (F). Google and Waze add the way to earn
trust in a judge: run A-A tests, feed it known-bad versions, and keep the canary "non-blocking" with manual judgment
until it has proved itself; Waze credits canaries with preventing about a quarter of its incidents (F). The SRE
workbook's line is the one for this lab: "terminating a canary deployment after receiving just a handful of queries
doesn't provide a useful signal" (F), and practitioners add that "a zero percent error rate over three requests is not
strong evidence", that promotion logic needs minimum request counts, and that synthetic-only canaries "miss
data-dependent failures" (S). The arithmetic is unforgiving: telling a 0.1 percent error rate from 0.2 percent takes
about 30,000 requests on each side (S). Amazon's Lambda documentation says the same thing from the other end: "at low
traffic levels, you might see a high variance between the configured and actual percentage of traffic" (F).

**Durations.** The published defaults are all longer than five minutes: AWS CodeDeploy's named configurations are 10
percent for 5, 10, 15 or 30 minutes, or 10 percent every 1, 2, 3 or 10 minutes (F); Amazon ECS's new built-in canary
(October 2025) defaults to 5 percent with a 10-minute bake and linear steps of 10 percent with 6-minute bakes, each stage
at most 24 hours (F); Microsoft's safe deployment practices say "a 24-hour day should be enough time for most scenarios
to expose latent bugs", including a peak-usage period (F); Facebook's classic shape is employees, then 2 percent, then
everyone "over a few hours", with push-blocking alerts and an emergency stop (F). Every one of these also rolls back on
an alarm that fires after the bake: CodeDeploy watches up to ten CloudWatch alarms and, fail-closed, "stops if alarm
status information can't be retrieved" (F).

**Google's own path for Cloud Run has caught up.** Cloud Run itself still documents no canary and no automatic rollback:
deploy with no traffic, move a percentage, and a rollback "changes routing immediately" with no health re-check (F); HTTP
readiness probes became generally available on 2026-06-29 (F). Cloud Deploy is the documented way to more: canary phases
by percentage (its example 25, 50, 75), an automation rule that advances a phase after a wait of up to 14 days, a
"repair rollout" rule with retries and then rollback (GA 2025-03-31), and since 2026-03-23 an analysis job that watches
"Google Cloud Observability alerting policies" or a custom container for a configured duration: "if an alert is
triggered ... the analysis job fails and so does your rollout", and the repair rule rolls it back (F). The first
pipeline is free. The roadmap's condition for it, "if the runner's split release ever proves too little", is unchanged,
but the gap it would close is now specific: a bake measured in hours against the alert policies the platform already
creates, instead of minutes against raw logs.

**Deployment markers: what the others show after a deploy.** Datadog records a deployment with its version and commit,
compares any two versions on requests, error rate and p50 to p99 latency, detects a rollback when "a previously deployed
version is redeployed", and its Watchdog flags a faulty deployment "within minutes" from new error types or a raised
error rate, skipping the verdict when errors are "few and transient" or no earlier deployment gives a baseline (F). New
Relic's change tracking shows "percentage changes in key metrics like throughput, error rate, and response time" over
equal windows before and after the change, and refuses to compute them until enough time has passed (F). Sentry's
release health is crash-free sessions and adoption per release (F). Grafana and Honeycomb mark deploys on every chart
(F). The common idea is a before-and-after per release, computed once enough time and traffic have passed, with "not
enough yet" as an honest state.

**Gates on the GitHub side.** Environments offer required reviewers, a wait timer of up to 30 days and branch rules
(F); custom protection rules, where an app such as Datadog or Honeycomb approves or rejects a deployment, are still a
public preview and need GitHub Enterprise Cloud (F). Their defaults are instructive anyway: Datadog rejects on ALERT,
WARN or NODATA; Honeycomb's example fails a build on more than three 500s in staging and more than one in production
over 30 minutes (F).

**The hosted platforms do less than this platform.** Render waits up to 15 minutes for a health check and cancels the
deploy if it never passes, pulls an instance after 15 seconds of failures, and rolls back by hand (F); Railway checks
health "only at deploy time" (F); Fly.io has rolling, canary and blue-green strategies but "no special rollback
command" and no automatic rollback (F); Heroku switches routing about three minutes after a deploy with no health check
(F); Vercel's rolling releases (GA 2025-06-25) send a fraction to the new build, show an error comparison, and advance
by hand or automatically (F). None of them watches real traffic after the switch and restores the previous version on
its own; the platform's bake is already ahead of the field it was compared with on 2026-10-20.

### 5.2 Objectives, error budgets and alerts for a service that is usually quiet

**The standard alerting shape, and where the platform's sits.** Google's SRE workbook recommends multiwindow,
multi-burn-rate alerting: for a 99.9 percent objective, page at 14.4 times the sustainable burn over one hour (with a
five-minute short window, 2 percent of the budget), page at 6 times over six hours (30-minute short window, 5 percent),
and open a ticket at 1 times over three days (six-hour short window, 10 percent); the short window is one twelfth of
the long one and only confirms that the burn is still going on (F). Datadog, Grafana, Honeycomb and the Sloth generator
publish the same table (F). Cloud Monitoring's own burn-rate page recommends two policies, fast at "10x the baseline"
over one to two hours and slow at "2x" over 24 hours, which is what the platform created; it caps the lookback at 24
hours, so the three-day ticket is not available there, and it warns that short lookbacks "can cause overly sensitive
alerting during low-traffic periods", offering only a larger threshold as the fix (F). The one-hour policy with no
short window and `duration 0s` means a single bad five-minute bucket can notify.

**The low-traffic problem is well described, and the answers are known.** The workbook's own example: a service with
ten requests an hour and one failure shows a 10 percent hourly error rate, a burn in the hundreds, and about 14 percent
of a 30-day budget gone at 99.9 percent; its options are artificial traffic (with the warning that synthetic successes
"hide real-user errors"), combining services that share a failure domain, reducing the impact of each failure, or a
lower objective and a longer window (F). Practitioners have refined this into rules a platform can code: show "N/A (0
requests)" rather than 100 percent when nothing was asked, and distinguish it from missing telemetry; at 99.9 percent a
28-day window needs about 2,000 requests before two failures are allowed, and with 200 "one failure misses the
objective"; keep every bad event in the budget but gate the page on a minimum number of eligible requests (the worked
example: burn above 14.4 and at least 100 requests in the window); or alert on an absolute count of bad events, or on
consecutive failed journeys; make synthetic probes "a separate SLI", not part of the user one; and "don't lower the
objective to reduce noise" (F). The Elixir library Parapet implements exactly the denominator guard (a minimum request
rate before any burn alert) and notes the six-hour and three-day windows are "more tolerant of low-volume variance" (F).
Nobl9 documents why its occurrence-based method "prevents false positives during periods of zero requests" while a
time-slice method counts an idle minute at 3 a.m. as bad (F); Datadog's metric-based objectives treat missing data as
good (F); Honeycomb says 15 minutes is "usually" too short a window and offers exhaustion-time alerts ("the budget runs
out in four hours") beside burn rates (F). Google's own implementation guide notes that a customer with very few
requests shows "100 percent or very low availability", so such services are tracked in aggregate (F), and recommends a
four-week rolling window with weekly summaries and quarterly reports (F). The SRE book's standing advice is "as few
SLOs as possible" and "start with a loose target that you tighten" (F).

**What Cloud Monitoring can do that the platform does not use.** Alert policies carry a severity (CRITICAL, ERROR,
WARNING) and user labels, which the notification can route on (F); the documentation block takes variables (the
resource's labels, the policy's labels, the condition's name), a custom subject line of up to 255 characters and three
"quick links" (F). "Evaluation missing data" can be set to treat absence as violating, as fine, or as nothing (F). A
metric-absence condition fires after up to 23.5 hours without data (F). Since June 2026 a PromQL policy can look back up
to two years, which Google's announcement illustrates with dynamic thresholds: 30 percent above the one-week average, a
z-score above 3, or half of the same period yesterday (F). Windows-based objectives ("good minutes") exist but "can hide
bursty failures" and weigh a quiet interval the same as a busy one (F). Google's library of recommended alert settings
has entries for BigQuery, Bigtable, Spanner, Trace and Logging and none for Cloud Run (F); the platform's thresholds (5
errors in five minutes, 2,000 ms, 80 percent memory, 3 instances) are its own and the research found no Google page
recommending them (U).

**Uptime checks.** Google's defaults: at least three checkers, the United States has three regions, the default alert
fires when "at least two regions report uptime check failures for a duration of at least one minute", the fastest
period is 60 seconds, and a check from three regions counts as three executions against the free million a month per
project (F). The platform's staging checks run every five minutes from every region, about six, so each service spends
about 52,000 executions a month; the free allotment covers about 19 staging services, and the 13 present use roughly
two thirds of it. Behind a sign-in front door the service-agent token works for programs; nothing official says whether
it passes IAP, which is why the platform accepts the sign-in redirect as "up" for people's pages (F). A February 2026
advisory found that the uptime check's service-agent token could invoke any authenticated Cloud Run service for a user
with only the viewer role; Google added permission checks (F). Checkly, Grafana and Better Stack sell the same check from
more places with escalation after two or more failures; Grafana's advice to run non-critical checks every five minutes
instead of every minute "cuts cost 80 percent" is the staging setting already (F).

**Alert design.** The 2013 philosophy still quoted everywhere: pages should be "urgent, important, actionable, and
real", "alerts that are less than 50 percent accurate are broken", sub-critical conditions go to tickets or a daily
report, and the rules are reviewed weekly (F). The SRE book's monitoring chapter: alert on symptoms, "every page should
be actionable", prune rules that are rarely exercised (F). Datadog's maxim, "alert liberally; page judiciously", with
three tiers, records, notifications and pages (F). The 2026 surveys of on-call teams: 80 percent say half or fewer of
their alerts are actionable, 44 percent had an incident tied to a suppressed or ignored alert, and 78 percent an
incident with no alert at all (F); the practitioner targets are fewer than two pages a shift, actionability above 80
percent, false positives under 15 percent, a paging path kept separate from digests, and unannounced notification drills
each quarter, after an outage detected at three minutes that a person learned of at 466 (F).

### 5.3 The observability stack, AI-assisted operations, and the evidence on trust

**Cloud Run's built-in telemetry is enough, and free.** Google's monitoring page for Cloud Run lists request count,
request latencies, instance count, billable instance time, CPU and memory utilisation, startup latency and concurrency,
and says "there is no charge for metrics on the fully managed version of Cloud Run"; request logs are written
automatically, one-line JSON on stdout becomes a structured entry with its severity promoted, and requests are traced
automatically at up to 0.1 requests a second per instance without charge (F). Everything the bake, the health view and
the weekly report read comes from there. OpenTelemetry is stable for traces, metrics and logs at the protocol level, but
its logs SDK is still "development" for Python and JavaScript and the Collector is "heading to 1.0" (F); Google's
Cloud Run sidecar guide still labels its sample `launch-stage: ALPHA`, and OTLP metrics are billed as Prometheus samples
(F). The roadmap's line, OpenTelemetry only "when an application needs traces beyond Cloud Run's own", stands. Two
additions since the last review are worth knowing: Observability Analytics (the renamed Log Analytics, GA June 2026)
answers aggregate questions such as a weekly p95 with SQL over logs and traces (F), and Application Monitoring with App
Hub's topology became generally available in late 2025 (F); neither is needed for a handful of single-container
services.

**The AI operations products, and what they admit.** Every major vendor shipped an investigating agent in 2025 and
2026, and all of them describe the same output: ranked observations, hypotheses with a status, next steps. Google's
Gemini Cloud Assist Investigations is still a preview, says results are subject to "the general limitations of AI
technology" and that re-runs give "small differences", and since 2026-04-10 requires a Premium Support contract to run
(F). Datadog's Bits AI SRE labels each hypothesis "validated, invalidated, or inconclusive" and publishes no accuracy
rate (F); Grafana's Assistant Investigations (GA July 2026) says plainly that "a higher tier doesn't guarantee a correct
answer" (F); AWS's DevOps Agent (GA March 2026) quotes self-reported "94 percent root cause accuracy" with no method
(F); Azure's SRE Agent (GA March 2026) ships a Review mode in which "the agent proposes an action; you approve or deny",
recommends it for production, and advises "start with review mode, observe for two to four weeks" (F). Honeycomb warns
that wrapping an API naively for a model produces "flooded context, hallucinations, and poor agent loops" (F); the open
HolmesGPT work found that runbooks, not a better model, cut wasted tool calls from 16 to 2, "telling the model what not
to do" (F). The measured side is sobering: on ITBench, agents "resolve only 13.8 percent of SRE scenarios", and the
strongest predictor of a failed run is "incorrect verification", with the advice "don't let the model grade its own
work" and to require tool-based evidence (F); a newer benchmark puts Claude Code around 60 percent end to end (S). Two
public incidents of 2025 are the cautionary tales: an agent that "deleted a live production database", fabricated data
and wrongly claimed a rollback was impossible, and a coding extension that shipped an injected prompt telling the agent
to wipe the host (F).

**What the evidence says about trusting automation.** Google's own practice note on AI in reliability engineering
defines five autonomy levels and starts agents at the one where "a human must explicitly approve any plan", advancing
only "for specific, well-bounded scenarios" after human-verified evaluations, with agent identities distinct from
people's and a "red button" (F); its January 2026 account of a simulated outage calls the agent "a copilot, not an
autopilot", gives every tool impact metadata (safe, reversible, destructive), and records "the agent proposes the
mutation, but [the engineer] authorizes it" (F). DORA 2025 finds 30 percent of respondents with "little or no trust" in
AI-written code and AI still negatively related to delivery stability (F). An operations survey of about a thousand
practitioners in September 2026: 99 percent want guardrails before an agent may act, only 24 percent accept full
autonomy, and the recurring theme is the agent "showing their work" (F). A 2026 study of 696 security analysts found
that reliance on automation shifts effort away from monitoring and that "systematic verification procedures act as a
mitigating factor" (F). Elastic's August 2026 guidance: "an approve button with no evidence behind it is not a control";
bind evidence, action, identity, time and outcome into one record; go observe, recommend, approve, then automate
narrowly; "never treat silence as approval"; widen autonomy only after measuring acceptance and rollback rates (F). The
counter-view from Amazon and Dell engineers: per-action approval "turns into rubber-stamping"; auto-approve
small-blast-radius reversible actions and escalate irreversible ones, because "the safety is in the blast radius, not
the signature" (F). The MCP specification itself says "there SHOULD always be a human in the loop with the ability to
deny tool invocations" and that annotations are untrusted unless the server is (F); Anthropic's guidance for agents is
checkpoints, stopping conditions and tools built so the wrong call is hard to make, and its own operating advice is "have
Claude show evidence rather than asserting success" (F).

**Code-written summaries.** No source measured the error rate of model-written status summaries against code-written
ones, but the practitioner consensus is to "shift all computation to deterministic code" and keep the model as "a
citation copy machine", and the MCP specification notes that a server's `structuredContent` is "unrelated to LLM
structured outputs" (S, F). Google's own example of operational reporting computes the p95 and failure rate in SQL
before any model sees them (F). The platform's design, a `summary` built by code from the result, is this consensus
already built.

**The diagnosis table is short.** Google's troubleshooting page for Cloud Run lists about 39 scenarios with exact
messages (F). The platform maps seven of them. Among those it does not: "The request was aborted because there was no
available instance" (429 or 500: the instance limit, quota or retries), "the instance could not start successfully"
(secrets access), the 503 tied to a liveness probe failure, the 504 of the request timeout, and the 5xx of an enforced
spend cap with its up-to-30-minute recovery (F). These are the failures of a running service, not of a candidate, and
the platform has no place to say them today.

### 5.4 What a trustworthy report contains, and how it is worded

**The numbers the industry reports.** DORA's current guide (updated January 2026) names five metrics in two groups:
throughput (change lead time, deployment frequency, failed-deployment recovery time) and instability (change failure
rate, "the ratio of deployments that require immediate intervention afterward, often a rollback or hotfix", and
deployment rework rate, added in 2024) (F). It publishes no elite-to-low cut-offs any more: the 2025 report replaced the
four tiers with seven descriptive archetypes, and its authors note that organisations had taken "a descriptive model and
treated it as a prescriptive scorecard" (S). DORA's own history page explains why reliability is not a sixth delivery
metric: it "is more a measure of operational performance than a measure of software delivery performance" (F). Google's
open-source Four Keys project was archived in January 2024 (F). DX Core 4 keeps change failure rate as the key quality
metric with failed-deployment recovery time and "operational health" beside it (F). Every one of these is a count the
platform already has in its database: plans, their final states, rollbacks and the time between a failure and the next
success.

**A baseline, then a regular look.** AWS's operational-excellence pillar asks teams to "establish operations metrics
baselines", because a number means nothing without the expected value beside it, and to review metrics on a fixed cadence,
inspecting "trends vs historic values" and outliers "masked by averages or medians"; AWS itself holds a weekly
operational review with a wheel that picks the service (F). Google's production readiness review covers monitoring,
emergency response, capacity and change management before a service is taken on (F). The commercial reliability reports
converge on one shape: Datadog's reliability overview scores a service by its average remaining error budget on a 0 to
100 scale (red below 66.7, yellow to 80, green above), trends it daily, weekly and monthly, and mails a PDF on a schedule
(F); Grafana's SLO reports are weekly (a complete Sunday-to-Sunday week) or monthly, one row per objective with a
sparkline, the remaining budget and each objective's share of the bad events (F); Sentry's release health is
"crash-free sessions" and adoption per release (F); Backstage, OpsLevel and Cortex scorecards are pass-or-fail checks
with named levels (F).

**What a post-deployment message carries.** Octopus's best practice: "always send notifications at the end of each
deployment", with the release, the environment, the status in a conditional sentence, and a deep link; notify approvers
when a run is "awaiting approval" (F). Render offers three tiers, only failures (failed deploy, unhealthy service, disk
above 80 percent, suspended), all (adds "deploy goes live" and "unhealthy service becomes healthy"), or none (F). Argo CD
ships triggers for deployed, health degraded, sync failed, with one message per commit (F); Flux events carry a severity
of info or error and the source revision (F); Vercel writes a commit status and a pull-request comment with the address
(F). GitHub's deployment status API holds a state, a 140-character description and an environment URL, and its
Deployments page shows the active deployment per environment with its history (F); job summaries take Markdown up to 1
MiB per step (F). Slack's own design guidance says to let the installer choose the rate and the channel, that "a chatty
app is not necessarily a good thing", and to offer "a digest of these messages rather than being alerted for individual
events" (F); the common pattern is one message per deployment, updated in place as stages complete (S).

**The words.** Google's incident-management guide: tell users and stakeholders "what's affected, how bad it is, what
workarounds may be possible, and when the incident may be mitigated and resolved", because "communicating consistently
and with an appropriate level of detail for the reader builds trust" (F). Its workbook adds: declare early, keep two or
three templates ready, and call the response off explicitly, since otherwise "people will assume the incident is
ongoing" (F). NIST SP 800-61r3 (April 2025) asks for procedures that say what is reported to whom and when, including
"regular status updates", and for each action to carry "the status and expected time frame" (F). PagerDuty's postmortem
template wants impact stated with "exact numbers" and an apology that is "genuine, not rote" (F). Atlassian's guidance:
communicate early "even before you know the cause", and always give "a time for the next update" (S). On tone,
Microsoft's style guide asks for "warm and relaxed, crisp and clear", writing "for scanning first, reading second", the
most important thing first, and its example is exactly the platform's problem in miniature: replace "Invalid ID" with
"You need an ID that looks like this: someone@example.com" (F). SAP's notification guidance: "communicate urgency and
importance without creating anxiety" (S). The federal plain-language guide asks only that content be written for its
specific audience and tested for understanding (F).

**Report back, or act?** The sources agree on the line. AWS: "the rollback should be initiated automatically on
pre-defined conditions", with "specific failure criteria" written down, and test-result reports made so a person can
decide quickly on the cases the criteria do not cover (F). Google's reliability engineers in 2017: "manual inspection
of monitoring graphs isn't sufficiently reliable"; a canary service should detect elevated errors, latency and load "and
roll back automatically (of course, this only works if rollbacks are safe!)"; first step 1 to 10 percent, at most
doubling per step, "at least 24 hours between release stages" (F). The SRE workbook: on a bad canary "pause and roll
back the deployment, or perhaps contact a human", with no fixed rule for which (F). And DORA's arithmetic cuts the other
way: an automatic rollback is still a change failure, so the platform's own count of restores is the honest stability
number, not something to hide (F).

### 5.5 Cost monitoring, anomalies and the cost report

**Where the platform sits on the FinOps ladder.** The FinOps Foundation's 2025 framework defines anomaly management as
the ability to "detect, identify, clarify, alert on, and manage unexpected cost events in a timely manner" and grades it:
crawl is manual checks that react "more than a week after it occurs", relying on budget alerts at project granularity;
walk is automated detection with "context-relevant thresholds (e.g., percentage change, spend ceilings, forecast
breaches)" routed by allocation metadata; run tunes thresholds per component and feeds post-mortems back into alerting.
Its KPIs are the anomaly count per week, the mean time to detect and notify, the cost avoided and the share handled by
automation (F). Reporting at crawl "shows actual spend and trend only"; walk adds "projected-versus-actual"; run surfaces
variance "the moment a threshold is breached, not only in periodic reports" (F). Budgeting's variance targets are 20, 15
and 12 percent by level, and the maturity model's own line is that "an organization's goal should never be simply to
achieve a 'Run' maturity in every capability" (F). On that ladder the platform's cost side is a solid crawl with pieces
of walk: labels enforced by Terraform, actual spend from the export beside list-price bounds, a weekly report, and a cap
that acts. What it lacks is the walk-level detector between Mondays, and a comparison with the previous period.

**Google now ships the detector, free.** Cost Anomaly Detection became generally available on 2025-10-30: it forecasts
each project's expected daily spend, "monitors your actual spend every hour", flags upward spikes "within 24 hours, for
most services", sets its cost-impact and deviation thresholds automatically from usage (the documentation's examples are
$200 and 20 percent) and sends email, individually or as a daily summary, or Pub/Sub; one configuration applies to every
project on the billing account; Google recommends watching anomalies "for at least one month before defining a
threshold"; it is "available at no cost" (F). Since July 2026 a preview of "early anomalies" covers Cloud Run and the
Gemini API with an expected latency of 20 to 40 minutes from usage (F). Budgets themselves send Pub/Sub messages
"multiple times per day", the first "several hours" after creation, and Google's own page on the disable-billing pattern
warns that there is a delay between cost and notification, that it "doesn't guarantee that you won't spend more than
your budget", and that resources "might be irretrievably deleted" (F). The native spend cap the platform already records
in staging reached general availability for Cloud Run on 2026-10-12: it measures gross list-price cost, enforces above
100 percent of the amount, "isn't instant", and a lifted cap can take up to 30 minutes to serve again, with no manual
workaround (F). AWS's and Azure's detectors are also free and also lag by a day or more; AWS routes anomalies to an
individual alert, a daily or a weekly summary by size (F).

**What a good cost report says.** Google's own cost-optimization framework asks for "regular (for example, weekly) cost
progression snapshots", automated audience-tailored reports rather than spreadsheets, notifications on anomalies and
thresholds, labels enforced through Terraform, and relative metrics such as cost per transaction (F). The practitioner
guides agree on the contents: a comparison with the previous period, the top drivers, anomalies "flagged explicitly",
utilisation and waste, and a cadence of daily anomaly alerts, a weekly engineering summary and a monthly summary for the
budget holder; phrase findings as efficiency ("your staging environment has 40 percent idle compute") rather than blame
("15 percent over budget") (F). An anomaly alert states the estimated impact in money or percent, where, since when, how
serious, and a next step; a simple baseline is three standard deviations over a seven-day average, with known launches
excluded (F). Vantage's noise filters are a worked example of "gently": a day's cost at least $5, at least 0.5 percent of
the total, more than 20 percent above the trailing seven-day average, and at least 12 days of history before anything is
said (F).

**Right-sizing has no official number.** Google's guidance is qualitative: reduce CPU "if CPU utilization is
consistently low under peak load", raise it if latency is high; memory as standing memory plus per-request memory times
concurrency; the Cloud Run recommender advises only the billing mode and the security settings, "typically one day"
after a deploy; the sizing metrics are sampled every 60 seconds and visible up to 120 seconds later (F). The platform's
25 percent headroom rule and 40 percent CPU floor are its own, reasonable, and should stay labelled as such. Third-party
advice converges on instance-based billing above about 50 percent steady CPU and a maximum-instances setting of peak
plus half (F), which the report's billing-mode note already approximates.

**Two prices that touch this platform.** The Monitoring read API has charged $0.50 per million time series returned
since 2025-10-02 (the first million a month free; console reads free), which the health view, the bake and the weekly
report stay far under (F). More important, Google has announced that alerting will be charged at "$0.35 per month for
each metric reference" plus $0.50 per million points, "starting no sooner than September 1, 2027", with uptime,
billing and quota alerts exempt (F). The staging project holds 80 alert policies, 60 of them for ten test services; at
that price the staging alerts alone would be about $20 a month, the whole of staging's budget. Nothing is due yet, but
the test services' alerts are already noise, and the date gives the deadline for pruning them.

### 5.6 Making automation trustworthy: what the mature shops actually do

**Bake in hours and days, in stages, and stop on the first bad signal.** Microsoft's Well-Architected guidance on safe
deployment (updated July 2026) says there are "no low-risk deployments to production", that bake times "should be
measured in hours and days rather than minutes" and grow with each rollout group, that a rollout "must stop
immediately" when a group shows an issue, and, the point most relevant to a quiet lab, that usage metrics belong in
the health signal "so silence isn't mistaken for health" (F). Amazon's Builders' Library describes the same shape with
numbers: a one-box stage with at most 10 percent of a region's requests, at least one hour of bake after it, at least
12 hours after the first regional wave, two to four hours after later ones, a traffic minimum before a stage may pass
("at least 100 requests"), an aggregate alarm that rolls back every in-flight deployment so that "the rollback is
already in progress by the time the on-call engineer has been paged", and deployments blocked at night, at weekends and
while any alarm is active; the whole is "completely hands-off" (F). Google's SRE workbook on canarying asks that a
canary be "sizeable and last long enough to be representative", that metric intervals be no longer than the canary,
that the metrics come from the SLIs and number "perhaps no more than a dozen", and that the canary be compared with a
control rather than with "before" (F). Meta's Conveyor paper (OSDI 2023) reports 97 percent of container pipelines
fully automated, phases of 1, 10 then 100 percent, bakes of eight hours in some pipelines, a default that "a failed
health check triggers Conveyor to revert the release", and the figure that matters for trust: 5.4 percent of
deployments fail, but only 0.92 percent of finished ones are reverted or patched by a person (F). Meta's 2024 talk adds
that comparison thresholds "catch regressions better than static ones" and that a "pass percentage" should tolerate a
few out-of-bound points (F). Spotify's guidance on automatic rollback sets a stricter bar for the machine than for a
person (p below 0.01 against 0.05), notes that a false rollback "wastes time but doesn't harm users", and calls
automatic rollback "a safety net, not a replacement" that only catches the metrics you chose (F).

**Gates fail closed and are re-evaluated.** Azure Pipelines gates poll external signals until all pass in the same
interval and reject the deployment at the timeout (F); Harness verification offers "fail if no data" and recommends
10 to 15 minute windows (F); GitHub's custom deployment protection rules, where an app approves or rejects a run, are
still a public preview, while wait timers (up to 30 days) and required reviewers are generally available (F). Google's
own Cloud Deploy canary for Cloud Run runs a verify job per phase, fails the rollout when it fails, and advances each
phase by hand; it documents no automatic rollback (F). Kayenta scores a canary from 0 to 100 and sends a marginal score
to "a human approval path" rather than to either outcome (F).

**Automation stays inside a fence.** The SRE book's automation chapter: automatic procedures "can end up making a bad
situation worse", so they are "scoped over well-defined domains", must not rely on "implicit safety signals", and need
sanity checks, rate limits and idempotency; its Diskerase story is an empty list read as "everything" (F). Microsoft
treats every deletion as irreversible: validate inactivity over a full business cycle, snapshot, disable before
deleting, watch, then clean up (F). Google's data-integrity chapter asks for soft deletion with holds of 15 to 60 days
and for the recovery process to be tested continuously, with an alert "when a recovery heartbeat fails" (F). PagerDuty's
runbook-automation ladder is crawl (diagnostics only), walk (restart and verify), run (self-healing before a page), with
authentication, access control and audit logging as the guardrails (F). On Cloud Run itself, a failed startup probe
shuts the container down, a failed liveness probe kills it and the platform "limits instance restarts to prevent
uncontrolled crash loops"; Kubernetes' own documentation and the widely cited "liveness probes are dangerous" post warn
that a probe which checks a dependency restarts every container on one dependency hiccup (F).

**Drills are scheduled, not occasional.** Google's SRE teams run a "Wheel of Misfortune" weekly for 30 to 60 minutes
and company-wide DiRT exercises yearly (F); AWS's reliability pillar asks for game days "on a consistent, scheduled
basis" and names "documented but never exercised" as the anti-pattern (F); Google's architecture framework says to test
backups by restoring them in a non-production environment and to measure recovery time and integrity (F). Gremlin's
survey (2021, the newest found) associates frequent experiments with availability above 99.9 percent (F); the 2025 SRE
Report found toil rising to 30 percent of time (F); the Uptime Institute's 2025 analysis attributes most human-error
outages to procedures not followed or flawed (F).

**Watch the watchers with a heartbeat, not a silence.** Prometheus deployments ship an always-firing `Watchdog` alert
whose arrival is what an external service expects; its absence pages (F). Healthchecks.io and Cronitor make the same
thing a product: a job pings when it runs, a grace period, an alert when the ping does not come (F). Cloud Monitoring's
metric-absence condition can do it for any metric (absence up to 23.5 hours, but a series that never reported never
alerts) (F). Two GitHub traps are documented: scheduled workflows in a public repository are disabled after 60 days
without repository activity, and a scheduled run's failure email goes only to the person who last edited the schedule
(F). Google's federation troubleshooting lists the two token errors that mean a trust condition broke (F).

**Drift is reported, never repaired, by default.** HCP Terraform's health assessments "will not modify your
infrastructure" and leave resolution to a person (F); Argo CD ships with automated sync, pruning and self-heal all off,
pruning called "a safety measure" (F); Flux reconciles every interval but requires `prune` to be set deliberately (F).

**How trust is measured.** The SRE workbook names precision (the share of alerts that mattered), recall, detection time
and reset time as the four numbers of an alerting system (F). DORA's definitions: change failure rate is the share of
deployments that needed "immediate intervention" (a rollback or a hotfix), failed-deployment recovery time replaced
mean time to recovery, and deployment rework rate is the share of unplanned deployments caused by an incident (F); the
2025 report calls AI "an amplifier" and finds it associated with higher throughput and "increasing instability" (F, the
landing page; detail S). No published false-positive rollback rate exists beyond Meta's 5.4 against 0.92 percent and
Spotify's arithmetic (U).

### 6.4 The decisions

| # | Decision | Needed by | Default if nothing is said |
|---|---|---|---|
| D57 | The release report: when, against what, and what counts as enough | 23.1 | 24 hours after `succeeded`, the new revision's 24 hours against the previous revision's 7 days before the switch; at least 100 requests besides the health path on each side to compare, else "too little traffic"; "worse" means a 5xx share above 1 percent and above twice the baseline, or a p95 above twice the baseline, or an uptime pass rate below 99 percent; a report only, never a rollback |
| D58 | The quiet-service objective | 23.2 | Keep 99.5 percent over 30 days; burn alerts need at least 100 requests in their lookback; fast 10x over 1 hour with a 5-minute short window, slow 2x over 6 hours, a ticket-level 1x over 24 hours; the uptime check gets its own 99 percent objective; no synthetic traffic |
| D59 | Alert severities and channels | 23.3 | CRITICAL to email and webhook, ERROR and WARNING to the webhook and the digest; without a webhook everything stays on email; log-line errors are a digest line, not an alert |
| D60 | The digest's channel and hour | 23.4 | A comment a day on a living issue of the platform repository, at the watchdog's hour, plus the webhook when set; "all quiet" is always sent; the absence of a digest for two days fails the Controller watch |
| D61 | The trust numbers | 23.5 | DORA's definitions; a restore by the bake counts as a change failure; a suspected false positive is flagged, never decided by code; the numbers are shown, not used to change any threshold by themselves |
| D62 | The cost comparison | 24.1 | Previous period of the same length; a mover is flagged above 20 percent and one dollar; forecast = month to date scaled to the month; efficiency wording |

### 6.5 Later, and not planned

Later, with the reason: synthetic traffic during the bake, as an opt-in list of read-only paths in the contract, 100
requests over the window counted on a line of their own (the workbook's warning that synthetic successes hide real
errors stands, so the rules would still judge real traffic only; the hosted platforms do not do it, and 23.1 gives the
quiet service a verdict a day later instead); Cloud Deploy's canary with alert-based analysis and the repair rule,
generally available since March 2026, if a bake measured in hours against the alert policies is ever wanted (the first
pipeline is free; the roadmap's condition "if the split release proves too little" is unchanged); PromQL alert policies
with a one-week dynamic baseline (preview, June 2026) once a service has steady traffic to baseline; a windows-based
"good minutes" objective is not advised by Google's own page for bursty, quiet services.

Not planned, because the evidence is against it for this lab: an AI investigation product (Google's needs a Premium
Support contract; none publishes an accuracy rate; the platform's code-written diagnosis and summaries are the
consensus design); autonomous remediation beyond the three existing actions; a liveness probe by default (a probe that
checks a dependency restarts every container on one hiccup); OpenTelemetry or a tracing sidecar (Cloud Run's own
telemetry is enough and free; the sidecar guide is still alpha); a paid synthetic-monitoring service (Google's checks
are within the free allotment); lowering the objective to reduce noise; model-written status summaries.

## 7. Limits of this review

The live state was read at one moment on 2026-10-21 UTC; alert policies and channels were listed, but Cloud
Monitoring's API exposes no history of fired alerts, so how often each policy has fired, and whether any email was
opened, could not be measured; the only records are the validation record's drills. The 24-hour release report of
23.1 and the request minimum of D57 and D58 are proposals from the sources' numbers (Amazon's 100 requests, the
practitioners' worked examples), not measurements on this lab's traffic; the live exercise is where the thresholds get
tuned, and Meta's experience is that a year of tuning cut false positives from 12 to 3 percent. The arithmetic on
uptime executions and on the 2027 alert price is this review's, from the pricing page. Several sources could not be
read first-hand: the Netflix technology blog (403), Google's "Shipping safely" essay (not found), the full DORA 2024 and
2025 reports (too large to fetch; their numbers come from Google's own announcements and secondary write-ups),
Atlassian's incident-communication pages (navigation only), and whether the Cloud console offers a recommended-alerts
pack for Cloud Run; the search budget ran out before Checkly's gate and Gremlin's newer reports could be checked.
Vendor claims about AI operations products (MTTR cut by 70 percent, 94 percent root-cause accuracy) are quoted as
claims. Whether the operator has already turned on Cost Anomaly Detection in the console is unknown; the platform's code
and documents do not mention it, which is what the review measured. Nothing was changed anywhere.
